In 1996, McCullough wrote that the look and feel of computers is almost all look, and very little feel.

Miller writes about Moravec's paradox. Machines are good at abstract tasks like chess and Go. But they cannot match the perception and movement of a one year old child.

What AI makes best is the most visual part of architecture.

Which architect do we teach

What stays expensive

Miller asks if a machine can be creative. Among his seven hallmarks, he leaves "experience and suffering" as something machines do not have yet. He sees it as a problem that time will solve.

Now that images are free, what stays expensive until the end? I think it is the experience Miller left behind. It is the time a body has to spend. Sitting on a wooden chair for a real hour. Feeling your own voice get quieter as you open the door. No one can do this for you.

It is a good thing that anyone can now imagine a space with AI. What is in crisis is not space. It is the profession of the architect. If the value of an architect was in knowing how to draw images, maybe what we protected was not creativity. Maybe it was a monopoly on drawing.

McCullough says craft is not about how little we use machines. It is about how much we take part. I want to go one step further. Taking part has a cost. Education should not be a place that lowers this cost. It should be a place that makes us pay it on purpose.

This is an uncomfortable conclusion. It means that when everything gets faster, architecture education should get slower and more expensive. And slowness is not given to everyone.

So I want to ask.

One student made a better image. Another sat in that room for one hour. Which one gets the higher grade?
